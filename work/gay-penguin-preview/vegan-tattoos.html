Most tattoo shops are not vegan, and most people never think to ask

It surprises people, but a lot of standard tattoo supplies contain animal derived ingredients. Not all of them, and not always, but often enough that "is this vegan?" is a fair question with a genuinely uncertain answer in most shops.

The usual culprits are:

  • Bone char, used to achieve a deep black in some black inks.
  • Glycerin, a common carrier ingredient that can be animal derived or plant derived depending on the brand.
  • Shellac, made from a resin secreted by the lac insect, used as a binder in some inks.
  • Lanolin, from sheep's wool, which turns up in some aftercare balms and ointments.
  • Beeswax, common in aftercare products.

Even in a shop using vegan ink, the stencil paper, the green soap, the razor lubricant and the aftercare balm they send you home with can all quietly not be. Vegan tattooing is a supply chain question, not just an ink question.

How this shop handles it

The Gay Penguin uses vegan inks and supplies as standard. There is no separate vegan menu and no premium for asking, because there is nothing to switch. It is simply what is on the shelf for every client who sits down, whether they are vegan themselves or have never thought about it once.

You do not have to ask, or explain, or negotiate

Plenty of vegan clients arrive braced for a conversation about it, sometimes an eye roll. There is not one here. It is already handled, which is the entire point.

Which ink brands does the shop use? Naming them is a big trust signal, because vegan tattoo clients tend to know the brands and will check. Same question for the aftercare product sent home with clients, and whether the stencil paper and green soap are vegan too. Naming real brands would make this page far stronger than any competitor's version of it.

Does vegan ink look or heal differently?

No. This is the question we get most and the answer is genuinely boring: a good vegan ink behaves like any other good ink. The blacks are black, the colors are saturated, and the healing is the same. Ink quality varies by brand and by how it is cared for while healing, not by whether an animal product is in it.

If you have seen a faded or patchy vegan tattoo somewhere, that is a story about that ink, that artist, or that aftercare, not about vegan ink as a category.

Vegan piercing jewelry

Piercing raises the same question from a different angle, since the jewelry itself is metal but what is used around it may not be.

Are the piercing aftercare products and prep supplies vegan as well? If the whole shop is vegan across both services, that is worth saying plainly here and on the piercings page. If it applies to tattooing only, we should say that instead rather than imply more than is true.

Why it matters to the shop

A tattoo is permanent. For someone who has spent years keeping animal products out of their life, the idea of carrying one around under their skin forever is not a small thing, and being told "probably fine, I think?" by a shop is not an answer.

Being vegan by default removes the whole conversation. Nobody has to interrogate their artist, take a guess, or settle. That fits the way the rest of the studio runs, which is that you should be able to turn up as you are and not have to make a case for yourself.
